The Best Budget External Hard Drives in 2026 (When Storage Is Expensive)

Published August 3, 2026 • 9 min read • UK prices verified August 2026

Storage has never been less affordable than it is right now. SSD prices have surged by up to 50% since late 2025, Apple has raised Mac prices mid-cycle, and the AI memory crisis shows no sign of reversing before late 2027. All of which makes the humble spinning hard drive more relevant than it has been in years.

We should be upfront: if budget is not your primary concern, these are not necessarily the drives we would point you toward. For photographers, filmmakers, and creative professionals who want the best performance and reliability, our guide to the best external hard drives for photographers is a better starting point. But if budget is the main factor and you need the most storage for the least money right now, these are the five most trustworthy options available in August 2026.

Quick Picks

  • Best overall: WD Elements Portable 2TB - the most trusted name in budget storage, two-year warranty - £97.99 at Argos
  • Best value per GB: Seagate Expansion 5TB - still the best cost-per-terabyte option despite the price - £161.99 at WD Store
  • Best with encryption: WD My Passport 2TB - hardware encryption and a 3-year warranty - £104.99 at Argos
  • Best no-frills pick: Seagate Portable 2TB - includes Rescue data recovery service - £95.99 on Amazon
  • Cheapest reputable option: Toshiba Canvio Basics 2TB - the most affordable of the five at £76.99 at Currys

A note on pricing: All prices below were verified in August 2026 at major UK retailers. These are noticeably higher than historical norms - a direct consequence of the ongoing storage price crisis driven by AI memory demand and wider tech price increases. This is the reality of what external hard drives cost right now. Prices fluctuate, so always check before buying - but do not expect a significant dip any time soon.

What These Drives Are Good For (and What They Are Not)

Be clear about this before you buy. Spinning hard drives have read and write speeds in the range of 100-130 MB/s. A portable SSD will give you 500-1,000+ MB/s. The difference matters enormously if you are working directly off the drive - culling photos in Lightroom, editing video, running applications. For those workflows, an SSD is still the right tool.

Where HDDs still make complete sense:

  • Backup drives that sit on a shelf and are written to periodically
  • Cold archive storage for finished projects
  • A second or third copy of something you want to keep but rarely access
  • Offloading completed client work to free up faster working drives

Given that we have covered the SSD price surge in detail elsewhere, the case for keeping a couple of good HDDs for archive work has arguably never been stronger. Here are the best options right now.

The WD Elements has been the reliable default in the budget HDD category for years, and it earns that position through consistent performance and an above-average warranty. While most budget competitors offer just 12 months of coverage, Western Digital gives you two years - a meaningful difference when you are trusting a drive with backup copies of important work.

The drive comes formatted for Windows out of the box, but reformats easily to macOS APFS or exFAT for cross-platform use. No bundled software to install, no mandatory apps, no account creation. Plug it in, it mounts, you copy files. The matte black plastic shell is unremarkable but feels solid enough for desk or bag use.

WD's reliability data holds up well across independent monitoring. Backblaze, which publishes quarterly failure rate data for drives it uses in its own data centres, has consistently found WD among the more reliable brands in the HDD category. That matters more than most spec sheet numbers when you are choosing a backup drive.

Best for: Anyone who wants a trustworthy, no-nonsense backup or archive drive from a brand with a strong reliability record. The two-year warranty over competitors' one-year coverage is the deciding factor at this price point.

The maths on the Seagate Expansion 5TB are difficult to argue with. At around $90 for 5TB, you are getting approximately $0.018 per gigabyte - roughly half the cost per GB of the 2TB options. If your priority is fitting as much data as possible onto a drive for the least amount of money, this is your answer.

It is worth being honest about the trade-offs. The 5TB portable Seagate is physically larger than the 2TB models - it is noticeably thicker and heavier, which makes it less ideal for travel but perfectly fine for a desk setup. The one-year warranty is shorter than the WD Elements' two years. And while Seagate's reliability has improved significantly, WD holds a slight historical edge in some independent monitoring reports.

None of that matters much if this is going to sit on a shelf as an archive drive. At this price per terabyte, you can afford to have two of them - one primary archive, one backup copy - and still spend less than you would on a single 2TB SSD.

Best for: Anyone building a large archive who needs maximum capacity per pound spent. Photographers and filmmakers with years of completed work to archive will find the 5TB capacity genuinely useful.

The My Passport sits one rung above the WD Elements in Western Digital's own lineup, and the most meaningful upgrade is hardware encryption. The drive includes 256-bit AES hardware encryption managed through WD's software - not the kind that is easy to bypass, but proper on-disk encryption that protects data if the drive is lost or stolen.

For photographers and videographers storing client deliverables, this is a meaningful feature at a price point that barely asks you to stretch. A three-year warranty (vs. two on the Elements) and WD Backup software are also included. The drive is available in several colours, which sounds like a small thing until you are trying to identify which drive is which on a shelf of identical black rectangles.

The $10 premium over the WD Elements is well justified if security or the longer warranty matters to you. If neither does, the Elements saves you the difference.

Best for: Photographers, videographers, and freelancers who store client work and want password protection on their archive drive as a basic professional precaution.

The Seagate Portable is frequently the cheapest 2TB drive from a major brand you will find at any given moment, and it comes with a feature that punches above its price: Seagate Rescue Data Recovery. Included for one year with purchase, this service can recover data from a physically damaged drive - flood, fire, mechanical failure - through Seagate's professional recovery service. For a backup drive, that is a surprisingly reassuring inclusion.

The warranty is one year rather than WD's two, and the build feels a step below the WD Elements in finish quality - the glossy plastic shows fingerprints and scratches more readily. It is also slightly slower in sustained transfers. None of these are dealbreakers for a drive you are using primarily for archive copies, but they are worth knowing before you choose.

Compatible with Mac, Windows, PlayStation, and Xbox straight out of the box. Plug and play, no software required.

Best for: Budget-first buyers who want the cheapest 2TB from a reputable brand, with the added safety net of Seagate's Rescue data recovery service included.

The Canvio Basics exists for one reason: to be the cheapest 2TB drive from a brand you have heard of. It does that job well. The matte finish is actually a step up cosmetically from the glossy Seagate Portable - it resists fingerprints and looks tidier on a desk - but in almost every other respect it is the most stripped-back option here.

No backup software. No encryption. No recovery service. A USB 3.0 connection (not USB-C, which means you will need an adapter for most modern Macs). A two-year warranty is included, which is better than the Seagate Portable, but the drive does not match WD's reliability record in independent long-term monitoring.

The Canvio Basics earns its place on this list for students, casual users, and anyone who simply needs a cheap extra copy of something and does not want to spend more than the absolute minimum on a drive from a known brand. For professional archive use, we would push you toward the WD Elements at a few dollars more.

Best for: Entry-level users, students, or anyone who needs a very cheap second copy of files from a brand with a reasonable track record. Not our first recommendation for professional archive work.
